CASE STUDY / OPERATIONS × PRODUCT × ENGINEERING

Free Food
For All.

Designing the infrastructure behind food aid.

A connected inventory and distribution management system supporting the operational work behind getting food aid to the people who need it.

MY ROLELead Designer · System Architect · Full-Stack Developer
TYPEOperational platform · Inventory and distribution
STATUSDeployed · In operational use

01 / THE CONTEXT

Food aid doesn't end at a donation.

Behind each delivery is a chain of coordination: suppliers and donors, stock intake, programme allocation, beneficiary records, warehouse movements and delivery outcomes.

Free Food For All needed an operational system that could represent these interconnected activities coherently, rather than treating inventory, programmes and deliveries as unrelated tasks.

The scope and flows below are grounded in the FFFA IMS user guide. Specific operational performance gains have not been independently measured for this case study.

02 / THE SYSTEM

Two supply paths.
One operational picture.

The system supports two distinct sources of food aid — purchased food and donations-in-kind. They have different intake and allocation steps, but both require reliable stock and delivery records.

PATH A / PURCHASED FOOD

Procure → receive → distribute

  1. Set up suppliers, items and packing lists
  2. Manage programmes and client records
  3. Generate purchase orders
  4. Record received stock and warehouse balances
  5. Generate and track monthly deliveries
  6. Return failed-delivery items to inventory
PATH B / DONATIONS-IN-KIND

Receive → allocate → distribute

  1. Set up programmes, zones and zone contacts
  2. Record donor sources
  3. Receive and consolidate donated stock
  4. Allocate donations to programmes
  5. Track outbound donated-food deliveries
  6. Maintain stock-out accounting records

03 / DESIGNING FOR OPERATIONS

Making each handoff visible.

The challenge was not simply to display stock totals. Each handoff needed a clear state, ownership and next action — especially where inventory accounting differs from real-time delivery tracking.

01

Procurement and receiving

Purchase orders, receivables and stock balances form a traceable path from planned supply to confirmed inventory.

02

Programme and beneficiary coordination

Client and zone records connect the inventory workflow to the programmes and people receiving aid.

03

Delivery exceptions

Failed deliveries are not a dead end. The workflow routes undelivered items back through an unpacking and stock-in process.

04

Operational versus accounting views

Outbound delivery tracking records delivery status, while 3PL stock-out records represent inventory drawn down for accounting.

04 / THE INTERFACE

From complex workflows to usable tools.

The hero visual is a portfolio recreation of a modernised IMS interface, based on the modules and operational information in the project. It is not presented as a screenshot of the currently deployed portal. The screenshots below document the actual operational interface.

The interface needs to help operational users find records, understand the current state of stock, and take the right next action without losing context.

05 / ENGINEERING OWNERSHIP

I designed the system.
And built it.

My contribution crossed the usual boundary between design and development. As lead designer, system architect and full-stack developer, I was responsible for translating the operational model into a coherent product and working software.

That meant considering the structure of records, relationships between workflows, data handoffs and what happens when real-world operations don't follow the happy path.

The navigation screenshot illustrates functional scope, not the underlying technical architecture. Technical stack and integration details can be added once verified.

06 / REAL-WORLD OUTCOME

Built for use.
Still in use.

The inventory management system was deployed and continues to support Free Food For All's operations. For this case study, the strongest verified outcome is that it moved beyond design and development into sustained organisational use.

The user guide also documents an online food-aid application workflow as a next phase. It is not presented here as an implemented capability.

07 / REFLECTION

Designing beyond the screen.

This project captures an approach I continue to use: understand the people doing the work, model the system around their actual operations, and design for the exceptions as carefully as the main flow.

Good experience design is not just what a user sees. It's also the structure that allows the experience to work.
