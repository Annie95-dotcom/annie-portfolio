01 — GROW with Singlife

Building Design as a Capability

This isn't a story about a wealth platform. It's about building design as an organisational capability — the same two years, at a completely different level.

May 2022 – Jun 2024
Senior UX / Product Designer
6
Product modules owned end-to-end
+20%
Onboarding completion improvement

The Product

A digital wealth platform for financial advisers, spanning Client Onboarding, Product Centre, Transactions, Dashboard, Reporting and Portfolio Management. Every initiative ran through the same discovery structure, benchmarked against iFAST and Fund Super Mart.

Problem Statement→ JTBD→ Success Criteria→ Baseline → Dream State→ Hypothesis→ Phased Timeline

The real problems were operational, not cosmetic: a 14-day manual trade-approval process, hardcopy account opening that still needed physical signatures, advisers defecting to third-party sites because internal filters weren't good enough, and no consolidated view of assets under management. The fixes — SingPass integration for verified data, SMS-OTP digital authorisation, automated validation and status notifications — pushed onboarding completion up 20%.

Design Capability — Built Alongside the Product

The product work ran in parallel with something less visible but more consequential: giving design a repeatable process and a way to report on its own outcomes, instead of running as an ad-hoc service function.

Brand Design System

Brand Guidelines→ Design Foundations→ Components & Patterns→ Visual Assets→ Templates

Covering iconography, a content writing guide, grid, mobile-responsive layout, motion, object style, accessibility and data visualisation. Alongside it, a companion Brand Design System website — she owned the IA, content production, hierarchy and interaction — built with the ambition of something people would actually want to explore, in the spirit of Klarna Design or Spotify Design. This is the work she's proudest of from this period.

Rebrand & Roadmaps

Led the GROW rebrand and a phased Website/Elevate redesign (Phase 0 live Q4 2022 → Phase 2 Q4 2023) — sitemap, IA, SEO/SEM and content alignment — plus a Touchpoints map governing legal-liner usage and naming across the corporate website, platform, social, presentations, EDMs, campaigns and stationery.

UX Design Framework

Scope & Define→ Ideate→ Design→ Validate→ Revise & Prioritise

A five-stage framework with named owners at every stage (Product, Design, Delivery, CX) and defined outcomes — the backbone for a roadmap running four parallel workstreams from December 2022 to August 2023, plus a "hygiene" track for the design handover process itself. 2023's design-function objectives (Design, Brand Design System, Project Enablement, Strengthen Usability Testing) each carried their own goals, outputs and success measures — a design team reporting on its own operating performance, not just shipping screens.

Stakeholders arrive with solutions, not problems. My role was to prove them right or wrong with evidence — end of the day, they are not the one using the product.
The constraint that mattered most

A white-label corporate architecture meant much of the intended journey couldn't ship — by her own account, "couldn't deliver even 50%" of it, against timeline, budget and development constraints.

Lesson: involve decision-makers and all stakeholders early, and know where to push and where to concede.

Recognition

All four awards below were earned for GROW's "Rebuilding Trust From Every Angle" campaign.

Gold — Best Event 100–500 Attendees, Marketing Events Awards 2024 Gold — Best B2B PR Campaign, MARKETING-INTERACTIVE PR Awards 2024 Silver — Excellence in B2B Marketing, MARKETING-INTERACTIVE Marketing Excellence Awards

The biggest thing this chapter changed: design leverage doesn't only come from better screens. It comes from giving design a repeatable process, a seat in how the organisation reports on itself, and the evidence to hold a position when a stakeholder arrives already certain of the solution.

02 — Propel, within Singlife

Designing Products With Users, Not For Users

Rebuilding a legacy, compliance-driven adviser platform — by removing the screen first.

Sole Designer
Product Owner, Business Units, Users, and me
3 × 3
Co-creation sessions, 3 advisers plus their managers each
600–800
Target users at internal launch

The previous system carried unnecessary flows and fields, and a generic journey that didn't reflect how advisers actually work with clients. The call was to rebuild rather than incrementally enhance it — "enhancing costs more, and it should just die off."

Process

Co-creation→ Gather Requirements→ Understand Current Behaviour→ Whiteboard→ Workshop→ Quick Mockup→ Quick Testing→ Prototype
Co-creation is scenario-based, on a blank whiteboard — deliberately no screens shown, to remove visual bias. Journey maps are a product of co-creation, not an input.

Three sessions, three advisers and their managers each, followed by usability testing with the same group — so the people validating the concept were the same people who helped shape it.

The stock challenge to stakeholders

"What are we actually solving here? Is this something the user finds troublesome, or something you find troublesome? Why are we changing for the sake of changing? What are the implications?"

On conflicting user needs: weigh them against business objectives, context and best practice, deprioritise rather than delete outright, and ask directly why a solution that works for one adviser doesn't work for another rather than assuming one of them is simply wrong.

Where it stands

Biggest constraint

Stakeholders getting anxious about prototype fidelity — the recurring reminder needed: "it's just a prototype, we're testing whether the concept works."

Validation so far has been positive — from advisers, board members and prospective clients. Not yet launched. Targeting internal use first, with roughly 600–800 users across multiple FA firms once it ships.

Products get built with users, not for them. Removing the screen removes visual bias, and surfaces needs that users often can't articulate when they're reacting to something already on-screen.

03 — BevEat

Building Beyond the Interface

Most designers work on interfaces. At BevEat, I worked across every layer of the product — from discovery and UX to architecture, infrastructure and deployment. Rather than treating engineering as a handoff, I learned how technical decisions shape user experience. This changed how I design forever.

2.5+ yrs
Dec 2019 – May 2022
20+
Projects across defence, health, food-tech and social service
Founding Partner
Product design + full-stack development

I joined a friend after our final-year project together — as a partner, to help get the company off the ground. What followed wasn't a single startup app. It was a run of connected systems built for defence, healthcare, social service and food & beverage clients, where I owned everything from product definition and UX/UI through to solution architecture, databases, APIs, deployment and security.

Product Strategy→ Research→ UX→ UI→ Architecture→ Backend→ Cloud & Security→ Deployment→ Support

Stack: Laravel · React · Node.js · Flutter · MSSQL / MySQL · Azure · Docker · Firebase Auth · Azure AD · SonarQube

Defence (RSAF, since 2019) National ePayments (NETS) IMDA Open Innovation Disability Sector Healthier Lifestyles
Before BevEat, I thought good design ended at the interface. Working across product, engineering and infrastructure changed that completely.
Hero Project
Live ordering & operations ecosystem

Bukit Canberra Hawker Centre

A hawker centre isn't just food — it's dozens of independent businesses sharing one physical space while trying to deliver a seamless customer experience. The brief wasn't "design an ordering app." It was: how do you connect customers, vendors, kitchen operations and management into one coherent platform, still in use by merchants today.

The system, not the screen

The story here is the connected loop: customer orders in, it routes to merchant kitchen operations, payment clears, the order is collected, and the whole flow rolls up into a management and analytics layer. Each hawker stall runs semi-independently, but the platform holds them together as one operating system.

Proudest moment

Presenting the Bukit Canberra concept to RC ministers — showing how the system supports individual vendors while giving the managing companies a single operational view. That framing, vendor-first with an admin layer built around them rather than on top of them, shaped how every later BevEat project got scoped.

Same Thinking, Different Domain
Social impact ecosystem

Free Food For All

Not a food app — a redistribution ecosystem. The same instinct that shaped Bukit Canberra (map every party in the system before designing a single screen) applied here to a completely different problem: connecting people who have surplus food to people who need it, with logistics and reporting in between.

A different domain, the same question every time: who are all the parties in this system, and what does each of them need to see?

Platform Thinking

The infrastructure underneath

Some of BevEat's most consequential design decisions never touched a screen.

PayEatFwd

An Open API PaaS coordinating the food industry, charities and supporting industries to minimise food wastage — separate login hierarchies for food providers and food receivers, SMS-OTP for postal-code-based users, merchant and SSA email authentication. Winner, IMDA Open Innovation Platform Food Rescue Challenge, 2022.

OpenAPIPASS & Single Sign-On

Built the architecture and foundation for OpenAPIPASS alongside the founder, including a Single Sign-On API endpoint connecting every BevEat product, and open-source contribution work including a NETS QR payment gateway integration.

A genuine architecture trade-off

Designing the API usage-tracking system meant weighing three different architectural options against scalability, tracking integrity, single-point-of-failure risk, and how easily each could interface with different databases — documented pros and cons per option, and a decision made before a line of interface design happened.

Hardest technical problem

Deploying for a CID client on an unfamiliar DSM server OS — extensive research, configuration and trial-and-error before a successful deployment. Not glamorous, but it's the kind of problem that shapes how feasibility gets weighed into every design decision since.

What Went Wrong

Lessons carried forward

ATOM Alerts — the core capability that never worked

The business problem was activating standby personnel simultaneously instead of through a sequential chain of phone calls. The team accepted the project without validating whether the chosen technology could reliably bypass phone silent mode to ring — documentation was sparse at the time. The surrounding features got built first. The core capability never worked, so the product couldn't fulfil its purpose.

Lesson: validate the riskiest assumption first. Your end goal cannot be your start goal — define the MVP around the core value proposition before expanding anything around it.

MVP scoping

"Super apps create complexity that we're not even sure users need — more to feed our own ego." The hardest product decisions at BevEat weren't technical, they were about what to leave out.

Today, I naturally think about architecture, feasibility and scalability before designing interactions — not because I'm an engineer, but because great user experiences are built on strong technical foundations. Technology evolves. Systems thinking doesn't.

Philosophy

The Lens I Design Through

"I don't start with solutions. I start by understanding context."

01 — Start Without the Interface

The first solution is often the purest one. Showing an existing screen makes people react to it instead of thinking about the problem. Design should begin with possibility before it negotiates with reality.

02 — Challenge the Problem Before Solving It

Not every requirement deserves a solution. Sometimes the best decision is that the existing solution is good enough.

03 — Design Is Contextual

There's no universal "beautiful." I cannot tell a fish how a tree looks from outside the water.

04 — Think in Systems

Isolated solutions work in the short term, but they have to fit the bigger business picture — or you end up building things that quietly conflict with each other.

05 — Find the Real Problem

People ask for solutions; what they need is help understanding the problem. Keep asking why.

06 — Start Narrow, Scale Deliberately

Your end goal cannot be your start goal.

07 — Design Is More Than Aesthetics

Plenty of work looks good and doesn't work.

08 — Technology Changes. Logic Shouldn't.

Stacks, platforms and tools will keep changing. The underlying reasoning — understand the context, find the real problem, design the system before the screen — doesn't.
